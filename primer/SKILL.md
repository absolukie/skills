# Primer

## Purpose
Generate a "primer" for any topic: one self-contained HTML page that hands the reader two things (his 2026-10-19 primer-skill-rebuild ruling, expanded by his note on the board): something to use, and new connections. The usable part lands as takeaway sentences and takeaway analogies the reader can carry away and apply: run the method, apply the playbook, make the call. The connections land through genuine interactions on the page, the kind that link this topic to things the reader did not expect (see the insight bar in The core floor). A primer is broader and more narrative than a mastery sheet. It never presents predictions as fact and never writes from training-data memory alone.

## Topics: he commissions, there is no gate (his 2026-10-19 note)
Anything can earn a primer, and Luke is the one who says so: he activates the skill ("write a primer on X") and picks the topic. Kit does not choose primer topics unasked yet. His note on the rebuild board, in full: "Anything. I will activate the skill typically. Like I'll tell you to publish a primer on X or Y. Maybe there will come a time when you will know what kind of primers you think I would like to read but don't know if I can trust you yet." Until he grants that trust explicitly, topic suggestions wait for an ask.

## Research: two lanes (his 2026-10-19 ruling, replacing rule zero)
Rule zero ("every primer begins with fresh research, no exceptions") is retired. This settles the refinement Luke flagged in his primer-process notes ("Research works for research heavy topics but not every topic needs research right"): research depth depends on the kind of topic, in exactly two lanes. Pick the lane before writing, and record the lane and its reason in the draft's feature map.
- **Lane 1, research-heavy.** The topic's facts are moving or contested: fast-moving fields, named voices arguing in public, claims and predictions that go stale. Run the full deep-research pass (`browser.deep_research`, or targeted `browser.search` passes for narrow topics) on the topic's latest developments and leading voices. The mission must ask for: current state of the art, landmark recent papers and releases with dates, and named expert views with attribution. Write from the research report, not from model memory. Record the research date visibly on the page ("Researched October 2026"); predictions and expert views go stale, and the date is what keeps the primer honest.
- **Lane 2, everything else.** Timeless topics (pure math, settled mechanisms) and first-hand topics (his own builds and checks) share the light lane: primary sources plus his own runs, no deep-research mission. For a first-hand topic the run itself is the evidence; any claim the run does not cover still needs a primary source.
In both lanes: if the material is thin on a sub-point, say so in the primer or cut the sub-point. Never fill gaps with plausible-sounding memory.
**Voices cap (his 2026-10-19 primer-cut-2 note: "Cap at 3").** At most 3 named expert voices anchor a primer. Pick the voices who carry the argument; a fourth voice, however eminent, waits for another primer.

## The core floor (his 2026-10-19 primer-skill-rebuild ruling)
Every primer, whatever its shape, hands the reader the same core. Never optional:
- **The mechanism.** How the thing actually works, at the depth the knowledge profile sets.
- **One worked example.** The machinery running on one concrete case with real numbers (see the worked-example rule under Operating rules).
- **Takeaway sentences.** The sentences the reader carries away and can use. Every section carries exactly one, set as its key line (see Built for skimming), and the primer's Takeaways section collects the payoff at the end.
- **Takeaway analogies.** At least one analogy that connects the topic to something the reader already holds, so the new idea has somewhere to land. The analogy and the real thing appear together (see Both registers under Operating rules); an analogy without its real counterpart is dumbing down.
- **Helpful interactions that produce insight.** Interactions on the page must produce a genuine insight, per his note's bar: they connect two unusual topics together, they surface what an expert level professional would say, or they yield a new abstraction. Interactions for the sake of interaction are banned.
- **The skim layer.** Key lines and bold discipline (see Built for skimming), so the argument survives a skim.
- **Sources.** Every primary source used, each linked; every factual claim traces to one.

Earned per topic, never automatic: voices and predictions when the field is live and arguing; a timeline when the topic has a real history; figures when a count carries the story.

## Shape: bespoke, built from the question (his 2026-10-19 ruling)
There is no fixed section skeleton. Each primer's sections are invented for its topic, in the order its argument needs: list the claims the primer must land, give each claim its section, order the sections by the argument, then write. His note asked for room for creativity and for each primer to feel fresh, not repetitive, so variety across primers is a requirement: check the shapes of the last few primers and do not reuse the same skeleton back to back. Recognizability comes from the visual system, not the skeleton. One piece of the close is fixed: Takeaways, then Keep exploring, then Sources, in that order at the end.
**Length (his 2026-10-19 ruling):** a primer is the right length when cutting any section would lose a claim. Every section load-bearing, nothing on the page only as decoration. No numeric budgets (settled on the primer-cut board; each sentence must have a meaning).

## Available moves (the retired anatomy, kept as a palette)
The old 8-section anatomy's pieces survive as moves to draw on when a topic earns them, not as a required skeleton or order:
- **The brief.** One paragraph that jumps straight into the meat (see Open on the meat under Operating rules): what this is, shown working rather than announced. It may open on a bold or outlandish claim, provided the primer backs that claim up by its halfway point. Ends with the single sentence the reader should remember.
- **How it works.** The mechanisms that drive the topic, as tappable cards or a click-per-step stepper. Each mechanism: plain-language explanation, one concrete example, and what changed recently. This is the engine room when the shape uses it; budget real design time here.
- **Timeline.** Landmark moments as an interactive timeline (horizontal scroll or stepper). Each entry: date, what happened, why it mattered. Landmarks only, no filler.
- **Key voices.** At most 3 leading researchers/practitioners (the voices cap in Research: two lanes; his 2026-10-19 note: "Cap at 3"), each with their attributed view in one or two sentences and a link to the primary source (paper, essay, interview). Include at least one genuine disagreement between voices; consensus-only primers mislead.
- **Where leaders think this goes (his 2026-10-11 call, revised same day).** He liked the contested/consensus framing but wants it clear who is on which side, so group predictions as explicit named camps, never abstract badges:
  - **Where they agree:** consensus items, each naming the leaders who share the view (e.g. "Amodei, Hassabis, and Altman all point to..."). No anonymous consensus; the names ARE the badge.
  - **Where they split:** contested items shown as named camps: Side A (names + their position, venue/date) vs Side B (names + their position, venue/date). Every camp lists its people; no "some say" without names.
  - Single-leader long-shot visions go under the split group as one-person camps, clearly marked as one person's bet rather than a camp.
  Keep the section title "Where leaders think this goes". Date-stamp the section and attribute every item (name, venue, date). Never present a prediction as your own conclusion. No new voices or facts beyond what the research supports.
- **Takeaways (his 2026-10-10 call).** A concise conclusive summary plus takeaways, its own section placed before Keep exploring (page order: Takeaways, then Keep exploring, then Sources). One short paragraph that states the conclusion in plain language, then 4 to 6 bullets with bold lead-ins carrying the memorable or actionable points. No new facts here, only the payoff of what the primer already established. **Word budget (his 2026-10-13 call: "the takeaway is way too wordy"):** the conclusion paragraph stays around 60 to 80 words and each bullet stays at one or two sentences. The takeaways are the part he reads closest; if a sentence is not carrying the verdict or a number, cut it.
- **Keep exploring.** Related primers with link-out icons.
- **Sources.** Every primary source used, each linked. Every factual claim in the primer must trace to a source here.

## Writing modes by domain (his 2026-10-17 call: "figure out different ways I like to write primers"; reframed as emphasis guidance by the 2026-10-19 rebuild)
Every primer is written in one mode, chosen before research starts. The universal layer (the two research lanes, the core floor, the bespoke shape, the sourcing rules, the writing principles, Built for skimming, the QA gates) applies in every mode. A mode is emphasis guidance, not structure: it sets the defaults the universal layer leaves open: how much math, what leads, where the emphasis falls, what counts as evidence, what the visualizers must do, and where the words may go. It never imposes sections; the shape is built from the question in every mode. Record the mode in the draft's feature map in one line ("Mode: AI. Secondary: engineering."). A hybrid topic takes one primary mode and at most one secondary influence, named in that line; never two primaries. Mode follows topic type first and tags second: an incident, a biography, or a product story is Other mode even when its subject is AI, and a physical system you can measure is Engineering even when it is tagged science. Tier defaults below plug into `~/workspace/user/knowledge-profile.md`; the profile, not this section, is the record of what he knows.

### Math mode (tag: math; the topic is a result, a structure, a theorem)
- **Leads with** a question or a problem. His five questions on the sine-cosine primer became its five expansion sections; the windmill primer leads with the problem itself.
- **Math:** the math is the content. A learning-tier or first-principles topic shows every step of the derivation, no skipped algebra; a solid-tier topic shows the key steps with real numbers. A theorem carries its proof (see the theorem rule under Operating rules). The worked example is the derivation run on one concrete instance (the bisected equilateral triangle, sides 2, 1, root 3).
- **Emphasis:** the derivation earns the length, in stages. When voices appear they are the genuine disagreements (a definition fight, a priority dispute). Predictions, where the shape carries them, are named open problems and camps. A timeline, if used, holds the result's own landmarks.
- **Evidence:** a claim is load-bearing when the primer asserts it is true, and it needs the proof or a derivation checked against the primary source. The characteristic fabrication risk is a skipped step dressed as obvious. Guard: any step the writer cannot restate in plain words gets researched or shown in full, never smoothed over.
- **Visualizers:** let him vary the quantity and watch the result change (the tangent line driven to 89 degrees, the circle-count slider on the pyramid primer). A spatial argument gets a drawn stepper, per visualizers rule 20.
- **Length:** the one mode allowed to run long, and only inside the derivation. Prose around the steps stays at fewest words; his sine-cosine correction cut density, never depth.

### AI mode (tags: ai, llms; the topic is a model, a mechanism, a measured result about one)
- **Leads with** a result. The brief states the finding and the one number or example that proves it.
- **Math:** default tier is fluent for LLM internals and agent systems, solid when the topic is the math under a model. Math appears only where it is the mechanism: at most one formula family per mechanism, and one worked example with real numbers carried all the way through. Already Clean kept exactly two pieces of arithmetic because the arithmetic was the claim. No math for color.
- **Emphasis:** the mechanism earns the length. Voices and predictions earn real length too when the shape carries them; the field argues in public and the camps are content. A timeline earns its place only for release histories, not for mechanisms (cut from both One Line Machine and Handwired in the 2026-10-15 rebuilds).
- **Evidence:** a load-bearing claim is a mechanism claim or a measured result, and it needs the paper, the official docs, or the system card. The characteristic fabrication risks are invented benchmarks and hype verbs. Guard: every number traces to Sources; a number that cannot trace is cut, not softened.
- **Visualizers:** run the actual tiny mechanism, not a cartoon of it: audit one real neuron, step the real arrows, run the tiny net on the page. Inputs are the paper's real values where they exist.
- **Length:** tight. Mechanisms and camps get the words; background gets a paragraph at most.

### Engineering mode (tags: engineering, infrastructure; the topic is a system, a device, a build, a failure)
- **Leads with** the system doing something measurable, or failing measurably.
- **Math:** numbers are measurements and tolerances, with units. Show the arithmetic that sizes the thing, worked with the real values: Wi-Fi Breath keeps the full line that turns a 5 to 10 mm path growth into 60.0 degrees. Formulas appear as sizing calculations, not as theory. Tier defaults to solid for control and physics topics, learning for unfamiliar systems.
- **Emphasis:** the mechanism earns the length, with the moving parts named. The worked example is a sizing or measurement walk. A timeline earns length when the topic is a program or a build with a real history. When voices appear they are the practitioners and the postmortem authors; predictions, when carried, are named roadmaps with dates.
- **Evidence:** a load-bearing claim is a measurement, a spec value, or a failure account, and it needs a spec, a datasheet, a postmortem, or the page's own measured run. The characteristic fabrication risks are a hand-waved mechanism with no moving parts, and a tidy result nobody measured. Guard: simulate the real system with real parameters and print the measured outcome, failures included; Ten Pendulums prints 0 of 300 holds at 10 poles as the finding. Label illustrative versus measured, always.
- **Visualizers:** a simulation with real parameters whose outcomes are measured on the page, or a measurement stepper: one diagram and one headline number per step.
- **Length:** tight prose; the words go to numbers, units, and the sizing arithmetic.

### Other mode (everything else: business, culture, life, language, faith, incidents, people)
- **Leads with** an incident, a claim to check, or a person's move.
- **Math:** none. No formulas, no KaTeX. Arithmetic appears only when a count carries the story, and then as a sentence or a fact-carrying visual, never as a worked panel of divisions. The 2026-10-15 rebuilds deleted Fix Your Life's whole math section and the Hugging Face incident primer's arithmetic panel; the census count itself stayed, as a dot grid, because the count was the story's scale.
- **Emphasis:** the narrative spine earns the length: the opening, the chain of events, the check or the twist. The mechanism is the few moves that matter. The universal worked example becomes a worked case: the claim checked against the record. Fix Your Life's replacement section is literally titled "The claim, checked".
- **Evidence:** a load-bearing claim is a dated fact about who did or said what, and it needs named primary reporting or the subject's own account. Voices are the evidence in this mode, so attribution carries the sourcing. The characteristic fabrication risks are forced quantification and padded background. Guard: if a number does not change what the reader concludes, cut it; background gets one paragraph before the story starts moving.
- **Visualizers:** a timeline, a stepper, or a comparison that carries facts (the incident's chain, step by step). The earn test bites hardest in this mode: a visualizer ships only if it carries facts the prose cannot, and none is acceptable when nothing earns it.
- **Length:** the tightest budget of the four. Fix Your Life's 48 percent cut in the rebuild is the calibration point. Background and throat-clearing go first.

### Where a mode and a universal rule conflict
- **Math amount:** the mode wins. The per-mode math policies above supersede the 2026-10-12 lean-toward-the-math call.
- **The worked example:** the universal requirement stands in every mode; the mode sets its form (a derivation instance, a mechanism run, a sizing walk, a checked claim).
- **Volume and density:** the universal fewest-words rule wins in every mode, math derivations included. Steps are content; commentary on steps is prose, and prose gets cut.
- **Everything else** (the research lanes, the core floor, sourcing, skimming, named camps when predictions appear, QA and verification gates): the universal layer wins.

## Sourcing rules
- Primary sources only for factual claims: the paper itself, the official docs, the person's own essay/interview, not a blog summarizing them. Link the exact page, never a homepage.
- Attribute every prediction and expert view inline: name, venue, date.
- Show disagreements between voices explicitly ("Amodei expects X by 2027; LeCun disagrees, arguing Y").
- If two sources conflict, say so instead of picking the tidier one.

## Build workflow
1. The topic is Luke's commission (see Topics: he commissions). Pick the slug, working title, exactly two tags from the taxonomy (ai, llms, engineering, math, infrastructure, design, business, science, tools, language, culture), the writing mode (see Writing modes by domain), and the research lane (see Research: two lanes). Never tag "interactive". Record the mode (plus the one secondary influence if any) and the lane with its reason in the draft's feature map before research starts.
2. Start from the primer reference implementation once one exists; until then, start from `~/workspace/blog-drafts/taylor-mastery/index.html` (canonical visual system) and adapt from it.
3. Run the lane's research, then design the shape from the question (see Shape: bespoke): list the claims, give each claim its section, order by the argument. Then draft the keylines before any prose (his 2026-10-19 primer-cut-2 ruling: keylines first): write each section's takeaway sentence and record the full set in the draft's feature map. Prose is written after, and it serves the keylines: a paragraph that does not support its section's keyline gets cut, or the keyline gets rewritten. Where the shape carries both voices and predictions, write those together (voices feed predictions). Then write the rest in the shape's order.
4. Keep the visual system identical across primers: paper `#f6f1e3` background, `#b3352b` accent, `.viz` cards, `.fcard` tappable cards, `.xlinks` keep-exploring grid, `.btn`/`.btn.ghost` buttons, verdict lede, meta tags, kicker close.

## Operating rules
- Re-read `~/workspace/skills/visualizers/SKILL.md` before building (slider spec, step-through rules: click-per-step, never autoplay; hide terminal stepper buttons with `display:none`, never disable).
- **Concrete worked examples** (his 2026-10-10 call). Every primer includes at least one fully worked concrete example: the actual prompt, the actual checks or numbers run against it, and the outcome. Abstract mechanism descriptions alone do not land; the reader should see the machinery operating on a specific case. Place it in "How it works" or directly after. Label it honestly when illustrative rather than measured.
- **If the topic is a theorem, the primer includes the proof** (his 2026-10-15 call on the windmill primer: "You didn't even put the proof in here though"). Examples and simulations show a claim working; they never substitute for why it must be true. When the headline result is a theorem or a proven claim, the primer carries the real proof (stepped and drawn where the argument is spatial, per visualizers rule 20), verified against the primary source (the official solution, the paper), with every step the source makes. If only a proof sketch fits, label it a sketch and say what it skips. A math primer that demos the theorem on examples and stops has not finished the job.
- **Every exhibit gets its own example** (his 2026-10-15 call, from the tense-grid primer: the sentence builder recycled the grid's "studying English" example, table and builder saying the same sentence twice). Different exhibits on the same page (a table, a builder, a stepper, a quiz, worked examples) each use a DIFFERENT example: different content words, different numbers, different scenario, so the reader watches the mechanism generalize instead of memorizing one instance. The single exception is a deliberate locked comparison, where the same example is run through every option precisely so the variable under study is the only thing that changes (terminology locked); that reuse must be the exhibit's visible point, it stays inside that one exhibit, and it never licenses the neighboring exhibits to reuse the same example too.
- **Math amount follows the writing mode** (supersedes the 2026-10-12 lean-toward-the-math call, which produced decorative math in topics that do not run on it; his 2026-10-15 correction: "adding math where there shouldn't be math"). The per-mode math policy lives in Writing modes by domain. The surviving core of the old call: where math is the mechanism, show it working, not just its name, and walk the actual arithmetic step by step with checkable numbers (one gradient step with real values beats a paragraph about gradients).
- **Both registers, never dumbed down** (his 2026-10-12 call: "don't dumb things down all the time. You can do simple analogies, both, go very deep into the most complex details. Do a bit of both"). Every primer teaches at two depths at once: the simple analogy or toy example for intuition, AND the unsimplified real thing for depth: the actual equations, tensor shapes, config values, and numbers from the real named system (a model's published config, the paper's own notation, a real artifact excerpt). A toy example must be paired with its real counterpart, each labeled honestly (toy vs real config vs representative values; never present invented numbers as a real system's actual activations). Simplifying without ever showing the real object is dumbing down, and he hates it.
- **Render the formulas in LaTeX** (his 2026-10-12 call, same day: "use more math formulas you can render in latex"). When the math rule applies, the key formulas appear as real typeset math, not prose approximations: vendor KaTeX inside the primer's own folder (`blog/<slug>/katex/`, copied from a sibling primer that has one, e.g. derivatives), explicit `$$...$$` display blocks for the headline formulas (the governing equation, the cost/size formula, the summation or bound that drives the argument) plus inline `$...$` where a symbol earns it. Every display formula gets a worked substitution right next to it: the same formula with the primer's actual numbers plugged in, carried through to the final value. A formula without its numbers plugged in is decoration.
- **Display formulas sit centered in their boxes** (his 2026-10-15 screenshot of the sine-cosine primer: two stacked limit equations rendered off-center in their equation box). Every display equation is horizontally centered in its equation box, stacked displays centered as a group, and the verification harness asserts each display block's center against its box's center within a few pixels at phone width. Left-drifting or group-shifted math is a publish blocker, not a nit.
- **The hero image must fit its column, and the harness proves it** (his 2026-10-15 screenshots of the drone and cartpole primers: hero img carried class "hero", a section rule, instead of "hero-img", the sizing rule, so it rendered at intrinsic width with most of the artwork off-screen right, while document scrollWidth stayed clean through overflow clipping). Every primer's hero uses the sizing class, and every verification harness asserts the hero image's own rendered bounding box fits inside the content column at phone width; page-overflow checks alone do not count as hero verification.
- **Define every symbol at the equation** (his 2026-10-12 call, from his screenshot of the KV primer's attention formula: "Always explain what the letters represent as close to the equation as possible"). Every display formula gets a symbol legend immediately adjacent to it, directly under or beside the equation block, defining EVERY letter and symbol in it (variables, subscripts, operators like transpose, the O in big-O) in plain words, before any other prose uses them. A paragraph later in the section does not count, and neither does assuming the reader knows the standard notation. If a formula's factors were just enumerated in the step above it, a compact one-line legend still goes under the formula itself.
- **Every visualizer earns its place** (his 2026-10-19 ruling, replacing the 2026-10-12 at-least-three floor; the floor is dead). There is no numeric minimum. A visualizer ships only if it carries what prose cannot: the mechanism running, the quantity varying, the comparison made visible, and it must clear the insight bar in The core floor (a genuine connection between two unusual topics, what an expert level professional would say, or a new abstraction). One great visualizer beats three dutiful ones; zero standalone visualizers is allowed when nothing earns one, and the feature map says why. Each visualizer must operate on the primer's real numbers, never lorem data.
- **Every mechanism card carries a visual or an interaction** (his 2026-10-15 call, from his phone screenshots of the rotation-matrices and pi-api primers: text-only cards in How it works read as unfinished). Each mechanism card's body contains at least one visual that teaches the card's own claim: a diagram, inline SVG, small chart, or mini interactive such as tappable chips, a toggle, or a click-per-step strip, operating on the card's real numbers. Prose plus an example box does not count. The card's visual is held to the same bar as every visualizer under the 2026-10-19 earn ruling: it must produce the card's insight, not decorate; a visual that only decorates is reworked, or cut with its card. The verification harness asserts every mechanism card contains a visual or interactive element, and the builder views screenshots of the opened cards before publish. New builds ship with the visuals in from the start; older pages get retrofitted when he points at them. A card visual may also be a Codex-generated illustration where an image teaches the claim better than a diagram does (his 2026-10-15 addition: Codex usage sits at 100 percent available, so there is plenty to spend); Codex is the builder's choice per card, welcome but never mandatory, and a diagram, SVG, or mini interactive remains equally valid. This rule binds every primer, including one already mid-build when a rule lands: a brief is a snapshot, and before ANY publish the builder re-reads this skill in full from disk and checks the page against the rules as they stand at publish time. (Added 2026-10-15 after the sine-cosine primer shipped with text-only cards because its build started before this rule existed and nobody re-checked at the gate; Luke caught it by screenshot, again.)
- **Open on the meat, no throat-clearing** (his 2026-10-19 primer-cut-2 package note: "Intro is pretty lame. I want it to jump right into the meat and potatoes."). A primer opens on the meat: the finding, the mechanism, the claim itself, never a warm-up paragraph about the topic's importance or history. The opening may hook with a bold or outlandish claim, and should when the topic has one, provided the primer substantiates it by the halfway point; a hook the page never backs up gets cut, not softened. The brief (see Available moves) is where this lands: it shows the meat instead of announcing it.
- **Built for skimming** (his 2026-10-15 call: "write these primers built for skimming... break up the monotony and emphasis on the most important sentences"). Three parts, all mandatory:
  1. **One takeaway sentence per section (the key line).** Every section gets exactly one `.keyline`: its takeaway sentence, the single sentence that carries the section and the one the reader should be able to repeat, lifted out of the text flow and set large (about 1.3rem, weight 650, accent left bar, generous margins). CSS: `.keyline{font-size:1.3rem;line-height:1.45;font-weight:650;border-left:4px solid #b3352b;padding:.1rem 0 .1rem .9rem;margin:1.5rem 0}`. The key line must be a sentence the section itself supports (lifted or faithfully compressed from its own content), never a new claim, a teaser, or format narration. Keylines are drafted first, before the section's prose, and recorded in the draft's feature map (see Build workflow step 3); the prose then serves them.
  2. **The bold skim test.** Bold the load-bearing phrase in body copy sparingly: at most one bolded phrase per paragraph, many paragraphs none. Reading only the headings, the key lines, and the bolded phrases from top to bottom must reconstruct the primer's whole argument. If the bold layer does not tell the story, the emphasis is wrong, not the reader.
  3. **No walls of text.** No body paragraph runs past about 3 sentences or 60 words without a break: a key line, a list, a visual, a card, or an h3 subhead. Any section longer than about 4 paragraphs gets h3 subheads. Enumerable content becomes a list, not a comma parade.
- **Writing principles, prose only** (adopted 2026-10-15 at his call: "your writing for my primers is not great"; distilled from a sourced research pass: `~/workspace/research_notes/primer-writing-principles-20261016-0503/report.md`). These govern the text itself; the visualizer and skimming rules above still stand. **Volume rule (his 2026-10-15 correction, after a polish-only rewrite still read as "word salad"): fewest words possible. Keep the most important sentences only; every other sentence must earn its place or go. Cut first, polish second; a shorter draft that keeps every load-bearing fact beats a graceful longer one.** **Calibration rule (same day): before writing, read `~/workspace/user/knowledge-profile.md`. Prose stays minimal in every tier; how much work gets shown follows the tier: fluent subjects state results with at most the one step that carries the idea, solid subjects show the derivation's key steps with real numbers, learning subjects show every step from first principles. Update the profile when a session proves a tier wrong.** Principles:
  1. **Common word wins.** If a plain word carries the meaning, use it. Needless complexity makes readers judge the author less intelligent and disengage (Oppenheimer 2006; Shulman et al. 2020).
  2. **Show the thing, name it last.** A definition does not neutralize jargon. Describe the mechanism in plain words first, attach the term as a label, then use that one term consistently.
  3. **Characters act.** Subjects name who or what, verbs name what they do. Undo nominalizations ("the rotation of the vector" becomes "the matrix rotates the vector"). Check: underline a sentence's first 8 words; you should see who does what.
  4. **Old first, new last.** Sentences start from what the reader already has and end on the point. Never end a sentence on a date, a caveat, or restated context (Gopen and Swan's topic and stress positions).
  5. **Subject and verb stay close.** More than about 8 words between them means the interruption moves after the verb or into its own sentence.
  6. **Example before rule, then fade.** One concrete, fully worked instance before the general statement (memory and worked-example research); then state the rule without the example so it transfers (Kaminski et al. 2008: concrete-only teaching traps transfer).
  7. **Cut interesting-but-irrelevant.** If deleting an anecdote, aside, or fact box leaves the explanation exactly as followable, delete it (seductive details, meta-analysis of 58 studies).
  8. **Name the wrong belief, then refute it.** Where a result is surprising, state what most people guess first and let the reader commit before the reveal. Clear exposition alone leaves misconceptions untouched (Muller's thesis experiments).
  9. **Open a gap, close it on the page.** By the second paragraph the reader can state the section's question in one sentence; the section answers it (Loewenstein's information gap).
  10. **Say it like a smart friend.** Final read-aloud pass: any sentence you would not say out loud gets replaced by what you said instead (Graham). Rhythm must vary; uniform sentence length is the AI tell, not a virtue.
  11. **Flagged as unsupported, do not use:** readability-score targets (Flesch-Kincaid and kin measure syllables, not comprehension), a blanket passive-voice ban (passive is correct when the topic continues or the agent is unknown), and shortness as a goal in itself (the evidence supports short distances and varied length, not short sentences).
- **Scroller/stepper hints state the gesture only** (his 2026-10-11 call). Hint copy says what the thumb does ("Swipe sideways.", "One tap per step, or use the arrow keys.") and never editorializes about autoplay or pacing: "No autoplay, your thumb sets the pace" is unnecessary. If the hint would merely repeat the section intro, drop the hint line instead of repeating it.
